C1 · What counts as learned was attacked 5 times: 2 survived, 3 narrowed. Status now: narrowed

Now known: after 3 narrowing attacks on 14 September 2026, C1 says “access the target under cue conditions” instead of “retrieve the target under conditions”; and “accessibility under that cue set, not mere study exposure or storage-without-access” instead of “the content is available in memory, not merely exposed”, and it gained 3 new bounds (Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Tulving & Pearlstone 1966; Morris, Bransford & Franks 1977).

Rules for learners and teachers

draft wording, pending Jonatan's review. Each rule comes from one claim and is no stronger than its current wording and bounds.

  1. Once something has been learned, spend review time recalling it from memory instead of rereading it. Check every answer against the correct one, and keep going until each item has come back correctly more than once.

    Only within these limits (6)
    • The payoff is on a later test, not one a few minutes away.
    • It does not rescue material that was never learned in the first place.
    • The practice questions have to match what must be remembered later.
    • Multiple-choice practice without feedback is excluded.
    • Low-stakes practice, not high-stakes testing.
    • The claim does not say the advantage keeps growing the longer you wait.

    From C0: Retrieval beats restudy narrowed 10 attacks: 5 survived, 5 narrowed

  2. Don't judge recall practice by a quiz a few minutes later. On an immediate test, rereading for the same time can come out ahead; the advantage of recalling is on a delayed test.

    Only within these limits (2)
    • The claim does not say how long "delayed" has to be (open question 1).
    • Same conditions as R1: learned material, matching practice, repeated correct recall with feedback.

    From C0: Retrieval beats restudy narrowed 10 attacks: 5 survived, 5 narrowed

  3. Count something as learned only when it can be brought back later, without looking at the material again, from the same kind of prompt it will be needed for. Having read it, seen it, or followed it while it was in front of you does not count.

    Only within these limits (2)
    • "Learned" is relative to the prompt: success with one kind of question does not prove it for a different kind.
    • Many classic retrieval-practice studies did not check this item by item; this is a stricter standard than they verified.

    From C1: What counts as learned narrowed 5 attacks: 2 survived, 3 narrowed

  4. While practising, don't use an AI tool that hands over finished answers or solutions. Practice scores go up, but less is learned when it is later measured without the tool, so check learning without the tool.

    Only within these limits (3)
    • Tutor modes that hold back direct answers are outside this claim.
    • Evidence so far covers secondary school to university; not yet shown for younger pupils or in Swedish classrooms.
    • Performance with the tool goes up; the claim is about learning measured without it.

    From C2: Answer-giving AI in practice alive Untested: no attacks yet

  5. When a continuous informational text must be understood in depth, paper gives somewhat better understanding than a scrolling screen on average. If it is read on screen, don't trust the feeling of having understood it; check.

    Only within these limits (7)
    • The average gap is small.
    • For narrative (story) text the difference is about zero or not significant.
    • Much of the gap is tied to scrolling; with paginated screens the differences are small or uncertain.
    • On tablets and e-readers the disadvantage is about half as large, and for schoolchildren it was not significant.
    • Does not cover navigating and evaluating sources online.
    • For children aged 1–8, story-congruent digital enhancements can beat paper.
    • Much of the evidence is from studies up to 2017 and from university students.

    From C3: Paper vs screen, deep reading alive Untested: no attacks yet
